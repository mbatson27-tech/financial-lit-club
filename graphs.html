The Graphs Students Must Know

Every member should be able to draw and label these four graphs from memory. Explore each one, run the shift scenarios, then use Test yourself to hide the labels and quiz yourself.

The Business Cycle
Dashed line = the long-run growth trend.

The four phases

  1. Expansion — real GDP is rising; unemployment falls and incomes grow.
  2. Peak — the high point of the cycle; growth stalls and the economy runs hot.
  3. Contraction / Recession — real GDP falls; unemployment rises. Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth is the classic definition of a recession.
  4. Trough — the low point; the economy bottoms out, then a new expansion begins.

The dashed trend line shows that despite the waves, the economy grows over the long run — mainly thanks to rising productivity.

The Production Possibilities Frontier
B, D, C (on curve) = efficient · A (inside) = inefficient · X (outside) = unattainable.

Reading the PPF

  • On the curve (B, D, C): every resource is fully and efficiently used. Moving along the curve shows opportunity cost — more guns means less butter.
  • Inside the curve (A): resources are sitting idle or being wasted — the economy could produce more of both goods.
  • Outside the curve (X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.

The whole frontier shifts outward when an economy improves its human capital (education and job training) or its physical capital (technological innovation). Try the toggle to see it.

A Supply and Demand Curve
Equilibrium: where supply meets demand sets the market price and quantity.

Why the curves slope the way they do

  • Law of Supply: producers make more as prices rise (the profit motive) — so supply slopes upward.
  • Law of Demand: consumers buy more as prices fall (common sense) — so demand slopes downward.
  • Equilibrium: the one price where the quantity producers want to sell equals the quantity consumers want to buy. Markets constantly push prices toward this point.

When something other than price changes — incomes, costs, fears, technology — a whole curve moves. See the Shifts tab.

How to read a shift

A curve shifts when something other than price changes. The old curve stays behind as a faded dashed line, and the equilibrium slides to where the new curves cross.

For each scenario, you need to know what happens to: (1) GDP Growth; (2) Unemployment; and (3) Inflation.
